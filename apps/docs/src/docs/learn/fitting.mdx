A layout has to work in a 1920px window, a 1280px laptop and a phone, with whatever panes the person opened. Jali never squeezes a tiled pane below the width its content works at. When there is not enough room, it folds a pane into a tab of a neighbour instead, and brings it back when there is.

## Three widths per kind

Each [kind](/get-started/key-concepts#kind) can give three widths. Each has one job.

<Code code={ticket} filename="ticket.tsx" highlight="8-12" />

- **`width.min`** is the narrowest the content still works at, once it adapts to a narrow box (with container queries, for example). It is the only width that decides folding: a pane folds when the minimums of what is shown don't fit, never because it would be below its comfortable width. A tab group's minimum is its widest tab's: every tab must fit when it is chosen, and choosing a tab never resizes the group.
- **`width.comfortable`** is where spare room takes the pane first. It defaults to the minimum.
- **`width.max`** is the widest the content usefully uses. It is soft: spare room past it goes to neighbours that can still use it, and only when every neighbour is at its maximum does the pane grow on. A split never leaves an empty gap. Leave it out for content that can use any width, such as tables and charts.

Heights have the same three, down a column: `height.min` decides folding as `width.min` does (Jali checks both after every fold), and `height.comfortable` and `height.max` say where spare height goes. A timeline that is right at about 200 pixels says `height: { min: 160, comfortable: 200, max: 240 }`, and the pane above it takes the rest of a tall window, with no weights to tune. Across a row, a row is as tall as the tallest its panes can use. A tab group is as tall as its tallest minimum, and takes its comfortable and maximum heights from its home tab (the tab it started from).

**Why a minimum, not a percentage:** a ticket at 200px is broken whatever the window. A width the content needs is a fact about the content, so the kind says it once, and every layout respects it.

## How spare room is shared

In a split nobody has resized, every pane starts at its minimum. Spare room brings each up to its comfortable width, evenly. Then it brings them toward their maximums, in proportion to their weights, and past that, still by weight.

With no comfortable or maximum width declared, this is simply a split by weight. A column shares its height the same way, by the panes' heights.

Once the person drags a divider, that split keeps their proportions exactly at any width, and comfortable and maximum sizes no longer apply to it. A drag can take a pane past its maximum. Double-click the divider to hand it back.

## What folds first

When a fold is needed, Jali picks one slot (a pane, or a group of tabs) to fold, checks again, and stops as soon as everything fits. It keeps the panes nearest the work on screen the longest.

The panes it protects are the focused pane, the pane it was opened from, and the panes opened from it. The order, first to fold to last:

1. In a [curated page](/get-started/key-concepts#curated-page), supporting views, but only one whose fold actually makes room.
2. Panes that are not protected and not kept.
3. Panes that are not protected but [kept](/get-started/key-concepts#linked).
4. Protected panes that are not kept, then protected kept ones.
5. A curated page's primary view.
6. The focused pane, last.

Within one of those steps, ties are broken in this order:

- panes with no links before linked ones;
- the far end of a chain of links before the near end;
- the pane focused least recently;
- the narrowest;
- the one earlier in reading order.

**Why this order:** a person working down a chain (tickets, a ticket, its customer) is looking at the end they are on. The far end of the chain, and panes unrelated to it, are what they need least right now.

## Where a folded pane goes

A folded pane becomes a tab of the pane it was opened from, so a ticket folds behind the list it came from, not behind something unrelated. If that pane is folded too, it joins the same group. A pane with no link joins its nearest neighbour.

A tab group is as wide as its widest tab needs. So a fold can make the group it joins wider, and that can push the next pane out too. A wide chart folding behind a narrow list makes the list's group as wide as the chart, and the pane beside it no longer fits. So when that home would still leave the panes too wide for the room, Jali tries the pane's other links and its neighbours too. It follows each through to a fit, and puts the pane where the most panes stay on screen. The pane it was opened from wins every tie, and whenever its fold already fits, so most layouts never see the difference. The choice depends on the room, so between two widths a folded tab can move from one group to another; the same width always gives the same arrangement.

The tab strip is for the tabs the person made. Folded panes don't appear as tabs: they wait behind one **N folded** chip at the end of the strip. Its menu lists them under "Folded for room", and picking one shows it in that place, where it is a tab marked with a fold icon. A folded pane is still mounted, so it goes on working: the chip shows the most pressing activity of the panes behind it, and each one's entry in the menu has its activity dot. The person doesn't need to do anything with them. That is what tells them apart from a tab they made or a pane they [tucked](/get-started/key-concepts#tuck): tucked panes are on the shelf, and folded ones never are.

## What comes back first

As room returns, the folds are undone in reverse: the last pane to fold is the first to come back. The same width always gives the same arrangement, so narrowing and widening the window again lands exactly where it started.

## Folds change only when the room or the layout does

Jali decides folds after a window or container resize, a change to the pane cap, a layout arriving from a link or Back, and a structural change such as opening or closing a pane.

Focusing a pane or choosing a tab never refolds anything. If clicking a folded tab pulled it out and folded something else, every click would rearrange the screen.

Tucking never unfolds anything either. The space a tuck frees goes to the tuck's home, and a reload doesn't spend it on folded panes.

## Opening when there is no room

If a pane opens beside another and there is no room for it, it joins the tab group of the pane it came from. The announcement says so: "opened as a tab: not enough room". The pane it came from stays on screen and keeps focus, and nothing else moves. Widen the window and the new pane unfolds beside it.

On a curated page the opened pane is the shown tab instead, so the link the person followed is in front of them ([Curated pages](/learn/curated-pages)).

## The pane cap

Even with room, Jali shows at most 8 panes at once by default. The person can change this in Settings, from 2 to 16. Past the cap, the oldest pane that is not kept, not focused and not a primary view becomes a tab. Every object stays open; only what is shown changes.

## One pane is quiet

Jali's chrome grows only when there is something to arrange. While one pane is on screen, with or without tabs, it fills the space edge to edge, with no border or focus edge, and its header has no tuck or maximise. If it has tabs, each tab's × closes it, so the header has no close button either. Open a second pane and both get their edges and buttons back.

## Maximise and full screen

Maximise lets one pane cover the others, which are kept behind it (⇧⌘↵, or the button in its header). Full screen goes further: the pane covers the whole screen, the browser's own bars included, for a chart, a document or a presentation. Its button sits beside Maximise; ⌘K has "Full screen pane, or exit full screen", and `jali.actions.toggleFullScreen(pane)` does it from your code. Escape, the button again, or leaving the browser's full screen puts everything back as it was. The pane is the browser's full-screen element, so nothing of your page covers it, whatever its z-index, and ⌘K, Jali's dialogs and the tab menus open inside it. Your own overlays, such as toasts, don't show until it leaves full screen.

Where the browser has no full screen, as on an iPhone, the pane is placed over the window instead. There, an element of yours around Jali's root that is its own stacking context (a `transform`, a `filter`, `isolation: isolate`) keeps it inside that element.

## Phones show one pane at a time

On a phone, where Jali's root is narrower than 600 pixels, it shows one pane at a time. That pane fills the width, with a switcher at the top listing every pane: ← and → move along it, and Enter shows the pane. It does the same at any width when even one pane is too wide at its minimum, since folding can't help then. Other panes stay mounted, so their state survives. In this mode a pane fills the width even when that is narrower than its minimum: there is nowhere else to put it. `createJali({ phone: { below } })` moves the line; `below: 0` keeps only the second rule.

### Two panes, stacked

A list and its detail often work best together, even on a phone. With `createJali({ phone: { stack: true } })`, a portrait phone shows the focused pane together with the pane it was opened from, one above the other, instead of one at a time. The two share the height as a column does: half each, unless a kind gives a comfortable or maximum height, so a short timeline stays short above its list. A divider fills the 24 pixels between them, so it never covers either pane. Dragged or moved with the arrow keys, it settles on a third, a half or two thirds of the height, and from then on the person's choice holds. The switcher marks both panes.

Stacking happens only when both panes have room: Jali's root at least 480 pixels tall, each pane at least 200 (`stackFloor`) and at least its kind's minimum height. It never stacks more than two. Held sideways, the phone is wide enough for the normal layout. While the on-screen keyboard is up, the pane you're typing in shows alone, so the keyboard doesn't cover it. A kind can stay out with `phone: { stack: false }`, or ask for its half with `phone: { place: "top" }`.

### Folding screens

A phone that folds into two halves, held half open like a book or a laptop, or a phone with two screens, has a hinge where content shouldn't sit. When the browser reports the fold, Jali puts a pane each side of it: the focused pane and the pane it was opened from, else the next pane, with a hairline on the hinge. Book posture puts them side by side, tabletop one above the other. Unfold the phone and the layout comes back. This is on by default, and changes nothing on a screen that doesn't fold. Turn it off with `phone: { fold: false }`. Browsers report folds in Chrome and Edge 138 and later; Safari and Firefox don't yet, and there the normal rules apply.

The layout, the address and the history don't change. Widen the window and the arrangement comes back.
